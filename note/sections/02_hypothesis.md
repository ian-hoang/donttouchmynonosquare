## 2. Economic hypothesis
**Who is on the other side.** Attention-driven investors who trade on a CEO's scripted verbal message and on-camera persuasion. CEO appearances on CNBC lift prices about 79 bp on the day and give back about 108 bp within ten days (Kim & Meschke); persuasive delivery moves capital independently of content (Hu & Ma, JF); analysts underweight negative vocal affect on earnings calls (Mayew & Venkatachalam 2012, JF). **Why it persists.** Involuntary vocal, facial and verbal leakage of concealed bad news (Hobson, Mayew & Venkatachalam 2012; Larcker & Zakolyukina 2012) is *soft* information: costly to process at scale (Engelberg 2008), arriving under limited attention (DellaVigna & Pollet 2009; Hirshleifer, Lim & Teoh 2009), and noisy per event (human lie detection is ~54%, Bond & DePaulo 2006), so no single trade is an arbitrage. The news then comes out gradually through guidance and the next earnings report.
**Hypothesis (written before results).** *Mega-cap stocks whose CEO publishes an interview with a high tell score (stress and verbal–nonverbal incongruence relative to the CEO's own past) underperform a beta-hedged benchmark over the next 20 sessions, and low-tell interviews do the reverse.* We measure stress against a person's own baseline; **we do not claim to detect lies.** **It fails if** the in-sample top-minus-bottom spread is ≥ 0, alpha t < 1, the effect is explained by the pre-interview run-up, a placebo built from folk cues (gaze aversion, blinking, fidgeting) works as well, or the out-of-sample sign flips.
